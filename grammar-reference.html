21–69 — a simple pattern

Tens + hyphen + units, except the "one" numbers, which use et un instead of a hyphen.

22 vingt-deux · 23 vingt-trois · 30 trente · 32 trente-deux · 40 quarante · 50 cinquante · 60 soixante · 65 soixante-cinq

21 vingt et un · 31 trente et un · 61 soixante et un (the "et un" exception)

70–99 — this is where French gets weird

French has no words for 70, 80, or 90 — it does maths instead.

70 = soixante-dix ("sixty-ten"): 70 soixante-dix · 71 soixante et onze · 75 soixante-quinze · 79 soixante-dix-neuf

80 = quatre-vingts ("four-twenties"): 80 quatre-vingts · 81 quatre-vingt-un · 85 quatre-vingt-cinq

80 keeps the -s on its own (quatre-vingts) but loses it before another number (quatre-vingt-un).

90 = quatre-vingt-dix ("four-twenties-ten"): 90 quatre-vingt-dix · 91 quatre-vingt-onze · 95 quatre-vingt-quinze · 99 quatre-vingt-dix-neuf

Ordinal numbers (1st, 2nd, 3rd...)

Add -ième to the number. Deux → deuxième. Trois → troisième. Quatre → quatrième.

premier/première (1st) is irregular — don't say "unième."

Tip ↴

  • À, au, en, and aux all mean "to/in" for places — which one you use depends on the noun's gender, not the meaning, so learn them with the place, not as a rule in the abstract.
  • For numbers, the 70–99 range trips up almost everyone — practise counting 60 to 100 out loud until soixante-quinze stops needing maths in your head.
  • Que and Quoi both mean "what," but que starts a question (Que fais-tu ?) while quoi stands alone or after a preposition (Tu fais quoi ?, à quoi bon).
